LESSON OVERVIEW

Trust & Goal Setting at Work

At the Rock Wall, people depend on one another to follow through. At work, trust grows the same way: people communicate clearly, complete their responsibilities, and take ownership when something changes.

Learning objectives

  1. Identify behaviors that build or weaken workplace trust.
  2. Explain how accountability supports a team.
  3. Create a SMART goal for a workplace skill or task.

National career-readiness alignment

  • Advance CTE Career Ready Practice: act as a responsible and contributing citizen and employee.
  • NACE Professionalism Competency: demonstrate personal accountability and effective work habits.

Explain what accountability changes for a team

Practice situation: Morgan promised to prepare 12 visitor packets by 2 p.m. At 1 p.m., Morgan finds that the printer has stopped. A teammate has access to another printer and can help if told now.

Name the problem and next step. Explain what the teammate can do because the update arrives before the deadline. This is unscored practice.

Compare your explanation with one possible response

“The printer stopped, so the 12 packets may be late. Can we use your printer now? I will assemble the packets and update you by 1:30.” The early update gives the teammate time to print the pages and helps the team meet the 2 p.m. commitment. Your wording can differ; explain the effect on the team.

Use the same idea in your own goal: your follow-through plan should let someone respond while there is still time to help.

Instructional reading summary: How to Set SMART Goals

SMART goals make an objective easier to act on and review. A goal is specific when it names the exact action or result. It is measurable when you can show evidence of progress. It is achievable when it is realistic with available time and support. It is relevant when it matters to the work, team, or career goal. It is time-bound when it includes a deadline or check-in date.

STEP 3.4 · PRACTICAL APPLICATION

Build Your SMART Goal, One Part at a Time

Estimated time: 20 minutes

Your next move: Study a complete example. Try one small revision. Then plan, write, and review a goal for your own career field.

Use a different task from the career example in Step 3.3, or change its target to fit your own time and support. Explain your own decisions rather than copying the example.

You can use a familiar task from a job, your career program, or a supervised practice setting. You do not need a job to complete this activity.

1. See how the parts work together

A useful goal tells you what to do, how to check progress, and when to review it. Your accountability plan tells someone else how you will show follow-through.

By the end of my fifth practice shift, I will use the station-opening checklist at the start of each shift and record all five completed checks in my log. I will practice with my trainer on the first shift and show the log to my shift lead after the fifth shift. This will help the next worker find the supplies they need.

2. Try one small revision

Starting goal: “I will use the station-opening checklist.” The action is specific, but we still need evidence of progress and a review date.

How many times will the worker practice? What will they record? When will they review the result?

Compare with one possible revision

“By the end of my next five practice shifts, I will use the station-opening checklist each shift and record each completed check in my log.”

The revision keeps the action, adds five recorded checks, and sets a review point. Your wording and time frame may differ. A complete plan also needs realistic support, a workplace purpose, and accountability.

3. Plan your own goal

This is the same selection used in Step 3.2. You can change it here. Changing the example will keep your writing.

Example task: Choose a career field for an example, or plan a familiar workplace task of your own.

Choose a familiar task that matters in your field. If you adapt the example, make your own decisions about the target, evidence, support, and deadline. Keep it small enough to practice with the time and support you have. Follow your program's safety rules and required supervision.

Write short notes in the six boxes. Each box asks you to make one decision. Use the hints when you need a starting point.

Finish “I will…” with an action someone could observe. Example: use the station-opening checklist at the start of each shift.

Name a count, target, or finished product you can check. Example: five dated checklist entries, one for each shift.

Name the time, tools, practice, or support you can use. Example: I have a checklist, practice time, and a trainer for my first try.

Explain how the task helps your team, customer, or career. Example: the next worker needs a stocked station.

Finish “By…” with a date or clear review point. Example: the end of my fifth practice shift. Avoid “soon” or “sometime.”

Finish “I will…” with what you will share, with whom, and when. Example: show my dated log to my shift lead after my fifth shift.

Need help choosing a measure or realistic target?

Try a count of completed checklists, a log of practice sessions, or a finished product checked against agreed criteria. Choose a target you can reach with your current time, equipment, and support. If a barrier makes it unrealistic, reduce the task or ask your instructor for help.

4. Put the parts into your own words

Write a complete goal and accountability plan below. You can write it yourself or use your notes to make a starting draft.

Optional sentence frame: By [deadline], I will [specific action]. I will track progress using [evidence or target]. To show follow-through, I will [what I will share, with whom, and when].

Your support and purpose notes help you check that the goal is achievable and relevant. Include those details in your final wording when they make the plan clearer.

This combines your action, measure, deadline, and follow-through notes. It does not check whether the goal is realistic or well written. Review all five SMART parts before using it.

5. Review the evidence in your draft

Find the action, measure, deadline, and accountability plan in the final draft itself. Planning notes alone do not meet those four response requirements. Use your support and purpose notes to judge whether the goal is achievable and relevant. Check each statement only when you can point to its evidence.

Revise before you continue: Replace vague words such as “better” or “soon” with details a coworker could check. If a part is missing, use the matching planning box to improve it.
